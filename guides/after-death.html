Educational overview

What happens after a death in Hindu traditions

Tradition variation: Timing, who leads rites, food rules, and which days are observed differ widely by region, caste community, sampradaya, and family custom. Treat this as a map of common patterns, not a checklist you must follow without a priest.

When someone dies, families often face both practical arrangements (permission, transport, cremation or burial per custom) and a sequence of rites led or advised by a priest. The goal of this page is orientation so you can ask better questions — not to prescribe outcomes.

Shortly after death (same day / next day)

  • Family and community are informed; elders or a priest may advise on immediate steps.
  • Body preparation and last rites before cremation or burial follow local custom.
  • In many North Indian contexts, cremation is common; some communities (e.g. certain South Indian or tribal traditions) have different norms. Confirm with your family line.
  • A priest may recite mantras and guide who participates.

The days that follow (often ~10–13 days)

Many families observe a mourning and purification period whose length and rules vary. Common themes (again: not universal):

  • Daily or periodic rites — offerings, prayers, or ceremonies on set days after the death.
  • Household restrictions — some families limit celebrations, certain foods, or temple visits for a period; others follow different rules.
  • Gathering of relatives — especially around key day marks that your tradition recognises.
  • Closing rites — often around day 10, 11, 12, or 13 (names and days differ: e.g. dasagam, terahvin, sapindi-related rites in some lineages).

After the first fortnight

  • Monthly or periodic remembrance may continue for a year in some families.
  • The first annual anniversary (and later shraddha) is a separate observance — see our Shraddha guide.
  • Some families seek Pind Daan at particular places or times; that is optional and tradition-dependent.

What this guide does not claim

We do not claim that performing or skipping any rite produces a spiritual result for the deceased or the living. DharmaSetu helps you understand typical sequences so you can plan with a priest who knows your tradition.
